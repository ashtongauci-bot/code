North of Wollongong, the suburbs squeeze into a strip of land between the Illawarra escarpment and the ocean. In the south, Bellambi, Woonona, Bulli and Thirroul have room for full streets and town centres. Further north, Austinmer, Coledale, Wombarra, Scarborough, Clifton, Coalcliff and Stanwell Park are villages strung along Lawrence Hargrave Drive and the railway, with houses stepped up the slope below the cliffs.

Almost every building job here is close to a neighbour, and often above or below one. A dilapidation report records those neighbouring homes before work starts, so any later claim can be checked against a dated, measured record.

What's being built

  • Knockdown-rebuilds and big renovations. Demand for coastal living has seen many original cottages replaced or extended, often with new footings, basements or garages cut into the slope. See dilapidation reports for demolition.
  • Town centre infill in Thirroul, Bulli and Woonona. Shop-top housing, townhouses and small apartment buildings near the main roads and stations put new construction right beside older shops and homes. Thirroul has its own chapter in Wollongong City Council's development control plan for the town centre.
  • Newer estates. The McCauleys Beach estate near Sandon Point, between Bulli and Thirroul, and other infill subdivisions bring new houses beside homes that are only a few years old.
  • Pools, retaining walls and driveways on sloping blocks, which often mean excavation into rock close to the boundary. See rock breaking and vibration and pool dilapidation reports.

Ground and site conditions

The northern suburbs sit at the foot of the escarpment, where layered sandstone, shale and coal measures are often covered by loose slope material. Slope stability is a real local issue. Wollongong City Council's development control plan has a chapter on geotechnical assessment of slope instability, and Transport for NSW has carried out repeated landslip and rockfall repairs on Lawrence Hargrave Drive at Coalcliff, Scarborough and Stanwell Park after the heavy rain of 2022.

For dilapidation inspections, that means looking well beyond the house itself. On a sloping street, a neighbour's retaining walls, stepped footings, subfloor piers, stormwater lines and paths can be just as exposed to excavation as their walls. Homes downhill of a site can be affected by changes to drainage, and homes uphill by cutting below them.

Closer to the beaches, the flatter land between the railway and the sea was historically wetter and more flood-prone, which is partly why Thirroul's early settlement started on the hills. On low-lying sites, existing slab and paving movement is common and needs to be on record before anything starts next door.

Housing stock we inspect

The area grew around coal and the railway. Thirroul's Excelsior mine operated from around 1900 until 1962, and the railway's locomotive depot opened there in 1917, so many streets still have early 20th century weatherboard and fibro cottages built for mining and railway families. Alongside them are inter-war and post-war brick homes, 1960s–80s brick veneer houses, walk-up units near the town centres, and large modern homes on rebuilt sites.

Coastal exposure adds its own existing defects: rusting steel lintels and wall ties, cracked render, mortar loss and corroded balustrades are common in older homes near the water. These are easy to mistake for construction damage if they aren't recorded first. For a guide to how they appear in a report, see how to read a dilapidation report.
