How to read a dilapidation report

Most of a dilapidation report is one long list of defects. Here's how it's organised, what each column means, and what to check when you get your copy.

Published 8 November 2026. Written by Integrity Inspecting Engineers, CPEng, NER.

A dilapidation report can run to dozens of pages and hundreds of photos. For a builder it's a box ticked on the consent. For the neighbour whose home was inspected, it can be a surprise to see every crack in the house listed in one place. Either way, the report is only useful if you know how to read it, and if you check it while there's still time to fix anything.

This guide walks through the usual parts of a report and explains a sample defect schedule line by line. If you're new to the topic, start with what a dilapidation report is and when you need one.

The parts of a typical report

Layouts vary between engineers, but most reports have the same building blocks:

  • Property and inspection details. The address, the date and time of inspection, who attended, the weather, and which areas were and weren't accessible.
  • The reason for the report. The nearby works, such as a demolition, excavation, pool or apartment building, and who commissioned the report.
  • A building description. How the home is built, as far as can be seen: wall construction, floors, roof, and any outbuildings, pools, retaining walls and fences.
  • Limitations. What the inspection couldn't see, such as areas behind furniture, inside wall cavities, under floor coverings or in locked rooms.
  • The defect schedule. The core of the report: a numbered list of every existing defect found.
  • Photographs. Each referenced to a defect number and, ideally, a location on a floor plan.
  • Sign-off. The name and qualifications of the engineer who inspected and prepared the report.

Why the defect schedule matters most

Everything in a dilapidation report exists to support one later comparison. When the post-construction inspection is done, the engineer goes back through the same schedule item by item and records whether each defect is unchanged, has changed, or is new. We explain that process in pre- vs post-construction dilapidation reports.

So the questions to ask of any schedule are simple. Could someone else find this exact defect again? Could they tell whether it has grown? If the answer is yes for every item, the report will do its job.

Reference number

Every defect gets a unique reference, often grouped by room or elevation (B2 for bedroom 2, EX for exterior). The post-construction report uses the same numbers, so B2-01 before can be checked against B2-01 after. A schedule without references makes that comparison slow and open to argument.

Location and element

"East wall of bedroom 2" is findable; "crack in bedroom" isn't, especially in a house with four bedrooms. The element column says what the crack is in, because the same width means different things in plasterboard, a cornice joint, a tile or face brick.

Description

The description records the shape and direction of the defect and where it starts and finishes. Direction matters. A diagonal crack from a window corner, a stepped crack following brick mortar joints and a straight horizontal crack can each point to different causes, and the engineer will want to see later whether the pattern has changed.

Size

Length and width are measured, usually with a crack width gauge, and written as numbers. Words like "minor" or "small" can't be compared later; "0.5 mm wide" can. Where a crack varies in width, a good schedule gives the widest point or the range. Look out for notes about offset or displacement too, as in EX-12 above, where one side of the path is higher than the other.

Photo reference

Each defect links to one or more photos, ideally taken with a scale or gauge in shot and from a position that can be repeated. That's how the post-construction photo can be taken from the same spot and placed side by side with the original.

What to check when you receive your copy

  • The date. It should be before any demolition, excavation or heavy machinery arrived next door.
  • Access notes. Were any rooms, the roof space, subfloor or outbuildings not inspected? If you can provide access now, say so.
  • Anything you already know about. Walk through your home with the schedule. If you know of an existing crack that isn't listed, raise it with the engineer before works start.
  • External items. Fences, retaining walls, paths, driveways and pools on the side facing the works are often where damage claims arise.
  • Photo quality. Make sure photos are clear and show where they were taken.

Don't be alarmed by the length of the list. Nearly every house has dozens of small cracks and marks, and recording them is what protects you if new damage appears.

What a dilapidation report doesn't tell you

A dilapidation report isn't a structural assessment, a building inspection for a purchase, or a repair list. It generally doesn't say what caused existing defects or whether they need fixing. Its purpose is narrower: an accurate, dated record that can be compared after the work.

Common questions

Why does my dilapidation report list so many defects?

Almost every building has cracks, stains and wear, and a good report records all of them, however minor. A long schedule doesn't mean the house is in poor condition. It means the baseline is thorough, which makes any later change easier to prove or rule out.

What should I do if I think something is missing from the report?

Contact the engineer who prepared it as soon as possible, before works start, and say which room and item you think was missed. It is much easier to add or clarify an item before construction than to argue about it afterwards.

Does a dilapidation report say whether my house is structurally sound?

No. A dilapidation report records condition so it can be compared later. It is not a structural assessment, a pre-purchase inspection or a list of repairs needed.
