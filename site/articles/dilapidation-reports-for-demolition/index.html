Dilapidation reports for demolition: what builders need before the excavator arrives

Demolition is usually the noisiest, heaviest and least predictable stage of a job. Here's how to get the neighbours' condition on record before it starts.

Published 25 October 2026. Written by Integrity Inspecting Engineers, CPEng, NER.

On most jobs, demolition is the first time heavy machinery works right beside the neighbours. An excavator pulls down walls and slabs, trucks run in and out over the driveway and footpath, and brick and concrete hit the ground a metre or two from someone else's house. If a neighbour finds a new crack a week later, demolition will be the first thing blamed, fairly or not.

A dilapidation report done before demolition is the record that settles it. This guide covers what builders should have in place before the excavator arrives.

Why demolition needs its own planning

Demolition affects neighbouring properties in ways that are different from the build itself:

  • Impact and vibration. Pulling down masonry, breaking up slabs and footings, and dropping debris all send vibration into the ground. Older brick homes, rendered walls, tiled floors and ceilings with existing hairline cracks are the first places it shows.
  • Loss of support. Removing a building can take away support that neighbouring structures were quietly relying on: a shared wall, a boundary retaining wall, a fence fixed to the old garage, or soil held up by the old footings.
  • Exposure. Walls, roofs and flashings that were sheltered by the old building are suddenly exposed to weather. Water getting in through a junction that used to be covered is a common complaint after demolition.
  • Truck and machine traffic. Heavy vehicles can crack driveways, kerbs, footpaths and stormwater pits, including council assets out the front.

Every one of these produces damage that could also have existed before the job. Only a dated record shows which is which.

Which properties to inspect

Start with the condition in your development consent or complying development certificate. If it names properties, those are the minimum. If it uses a distance or says "adjoining" properties, read it carefully and ask the certifier if it's unclear.

Beyond the condition, think about who could realistically claim damage:

  • Both side neighbours and the property behind
  • Any home sharing a wall with the building being demolished (semis and terraces)
  • Strata buildings next door, including common property such as car parks, driveways and boundary walls
  • Homes across a narrow lane or right-of-way that trucks will use
  • Council footpaths, kerbs, gutters, driveway crossings and pits along the frontage, where the consent asks for them

If you're unsure whether a property is worth including, the cost of one more inspection is small compared with defending a claim without evidence.

What the inspection records

A pre-demolition report should record each property's condition in enough detail that a later inspection can compare item by item. That means:

  • Every elevation facing the works, and the interior rooms closest to it
  • Existing cracks, with location, length and width, photographed with a scale
  • Walls, ceilings, cornices, floors, tiles, doors and windows that stick or don't latch
  • Boundary walls, retaining walls, fences, paving, driveways and pools
  • Roof and flashing junctions against any shared or adjoining wall
  • Council assets in front of the site, where required

A clear photo log is useful, but measured, located defect entries are what make the report work later. See pre- vs post-construction reports for how the two are compared.

Shared walls, semis and terraces

Demolishing one half of a semi or a terrace in a row is where the most care is needed. The party wall stays, but it becomes an external wall. Before demolition the neighbour's side of that wall, their ceilings and cornices along it, and their roof junctions should be recorded in detail. After demolition, the builder's engineer and the demolition contractor will be focused on keeping that wall stable and weathertight. The pre-demolition record is what shows its condition before anyone touched it.

Timing: when to book

The inspection has to happen before any demolition work, including soft strip-out that involves hammering or cutting near a shared wall. The practical lead time is usually not the engineer. It's getting access to each neighbour's home. Write to neighbours early, explain what the inspection is and that they'll be offered a copy, and give them a few times to choose from.

Don't do it too early either. If the inspection is months before demolition, the neighbour can argue that things changed in between. Aim for close to the start date, and tell your engineer if the program slips significantly.

Other things to have sorted before demolition

A dilapidation report is one item on a pre-start list. Others that builders commonly need to check before demolition include:

  • All pre-commencement conditions in the consent or certificate, and notice of commencement to council and the principal certifier
  • Asbestos and hazardous materials. Older homes often contain asbestos, and its removal is licensed work in NSW.
  • SafeWork NSW notification. SafeWork NSW requires notice 5 calendar days before certain licensed demolition work starts, such as demolishing load-bearing parts of structures six metres or taller. Check the current requirements with SafeWork NSW and your demolition contractor.
  • Service disconnections, tree protection, site fencing and traffic management

We only prepare dilapidation reports, so for these items speak to your certifier, demolition contractor and SafeWork NSW.

Neighbours: what to expect

If you're the neighbour and you've been asked to allow an inspection before demolition next door, it's in your interest to agree. The report records your home's condition before the work, and you can ask for a copy. If damage happens, that record is your best evidence. If you've already noticed cracks, see what to do if a neighbour's building work cracks your house.

Common questions

Do I need a dilapidation report just for demolition, before any excavation?

Often, yes. Where a consent requires a dilapidation report, it is usually needed before any work starts, and demolition is normally the first work on site. Check the wording of your condition. If it says before demolition or before any works, the inspection has to be done before the first machine arrives.

Can one report cover both demolition and the later build?

Usually. A single pre-construction report made before demolition becomes the baseline for the whole job, and the post-construction report at the end compares against it. If there is a long gap between demolition and construction, or a stage is added later, a check inspection in between can be worthwhile.

What if we share a wall with the house being demolished?

Semi-detached houses and terraces need extra care. The shared wall becomes an external wall once the building next door comes down, so its condition, the roof and flashing junctions, and the neighbour's rooms against that wall should all be recorded in detail before demolition.
