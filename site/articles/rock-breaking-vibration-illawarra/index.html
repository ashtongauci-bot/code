Rock breaking and vibration: protecting neighbouring homes on Illawarra sites

From the latite around Kiama to the sloping blocks under the escarpment, plenty of Illawarra jobs hit rock. Here's how to keep the neighbours' homes on record before the hammer starts.

Published 1 November 2026. Written by Integrity Inspecting Engineers, CPEng, NER.

Rock is close to the surface on a lot of Illawarra building sites. Coastal blocks in Kiama and Gerringong, the hillsides around Shellharbour and the foothills of the escarpment above Wollongong's northern and western suburbs all regularly need rock removed for footings, basements, driveways, pools and retaining walls. When that rock can't be dug out with a bucket, the excavator gets a hydraulic hammer, and the neighbours feel it.

This guide explains why rock breaking is one of the most common sources of neighbour complaints, what a dilapidation report should record before it starts, and how builders and neighbours can both stay protected.

Why rock breaking causes so many disputes

A rock breaker works by hitting the rock thousands of times. Each blow sends energy into the ground, and rock carries vibration further and more efficiently than loose soil. Neighbours often notice it a few houses away: windows rattle, crockery moves, and the house can feel like it's shaking.

That experience is unsettling, and it makes people look closely at their homes, often for the first time in years. They find cracks in cornices, render, tiles and brickwork. Some of those cracks may be new. Many will have been there for a long time. Without a dated record taken before the work, nobody can say which is which, and the builder usually ends up carrying the argument.

Illawarra ground conditions that matter

The Illawarra's geology varies a lot over short distances, and it affects how hard the rock is and how vibration behaves:

  • Volcanic latite around Kiama, Gerringong and Shellharbour. The dark, hard rock seen at Bombo Headland and along the Kiama coast belongs to the Gerringong Volcanics. It is tough to break, so hammering can go on for days, and sites on it are often steep.
  • Sandstones, shales and coal measures under the escarpment. The northern suburbs, Mount Keira and Mount Kembla foothills and the western edge of Wollongong sit on layered sedimentary rock, often under a mantle of loose slope material (colluvium). Excavation on these slopes needs care for the homes uphill and downhill, not just beside the site.
  • Slope instability areas. Wollongong City Council's development controls include requirements for geotechnical assessment of land affected by slope instability. On those sites, neighbouring retaining walls, stepped footings and drainage deserve a particularly careful record.
  • Shallow rock under clay. On many suburban blocks, a metre or two of clay sits over weathered rock, so a pool or garage excavation starts easily and then hits rock near the boundary.

Your geotechnical report will describe the actual conditions on your site. The point for the dilapidation inspection is that hard or shallow rock widens the area worth recording.

What should be inspected before rock breaking

Start with the properties named in your development consent or complying development certificate. Then think about where vibration is likely to be felt and where a claim could realistically come from:

  • Both side neighbours and the property behind, plus homes diagonally adjacent where rock is continuous
  • Older full-brick and double-brick homes, which tend to show cracking more readily than lightweight construction
  • Neighbouring pools, which can crack or leak, and their coping and surrounds
  • Retaining walls, rock faces and boulders on sloping blocks above and below the site
  • Tiled floors and wet areas, ceilings and cornices, and rendered walls
  • Council footpaths, kerbs and driveway crossings where the consent asks for them

The inspection should record every existing crack with its location, length and width, photographed with a scale, so a later inspection can compare item by item. For how that comparison works, see pre- vs post-construction dilapidation reports.

Dilapidation reports and vibration monitoring

These are often confused, but they do different jobs. A dilapidation report records the condition of neighbouring buildings before and after the works. Vibration monitoring measures ground vibration in real time while the work happens, usually against limits set by the project's geotechnical or vibration engineer, and sometimes required by a consent condition.

On a large rock job they work well together. Monitoring shows whether vibration stayed within the limits set for the job. The dilapidation report shows whether anything actually changed in the neighbouring homes. We prepare dilapidation reports only, so speak to your geotechnical engineer about monitoring.

Reducing the risk on site

Method choices are for the builder and their geotechnical engineer, but they're worth thinking about early because they affect neighbours directly. Options commonly considered on rock sites include rock sawing along the boundary before breaking, using a smaller hammer close to neighbouring structures, ripping or grinding where the rock allows, and breaking in shorter bursts. Telling neighbours when the noisiest work will happen also goes a long way.

Timing the inspections

  • Before any excavation or demolition. If the job also involves demolition, one pre-construction inspection before demolition usually covers both. See our guide to dilapidation reports for demolition.
  • During, if needed. On long rock jobs, or when a neighbour raises a concern, a check inspection can identify new cracking while it can still be tied to a stage of the work.
  • After the heavy work is finished. The post-construction inspection compares each property against the baseline.

Pool excavations are one of the most common rock breaking jobs in the region. Our guide to dilapidation reports for pool excavation covers those in more detail.

If you're the neighbour

If a builder asks to inspect your home before rock breaking next door, it's in your interest to say yes. The report records your home's condition before the work, and you can ask for a copy. If you've already noticed new cracks, read what to do if a neighbour's building work cracks your house.

Common questions

How far from the site should neighbouring homes be inspected for rock breaking?

Start with the properties named in your consent or certificate. Because vibration travels further through rock than through soil, it is often worth including homes beyond the immediate side boundaries, such as the properties behind and diagonally adjacent, particularly older masonry homes and homes with pools or tall retaining walls.

Is a dilapidation report the same as vibration monitoring?

No. A dilapidation report records the condition of neighbouring properties before and after the work. Vibration monitoring measures ground vibration while the work happens, usually against limits set by a geotechnical or vibration engineer. They answer different questions and are often used together.

Should there be an inspection during rock breaking, not just before and after?

On long or heavy rock jobs, a check inspection partway through can be worthwhile, especially if a neighbour raises a concern. It lets new cracking be identified early, while it can still be linked to a particular stage of the work.
